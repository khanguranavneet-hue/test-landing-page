Lesson: Stars

Reading the pause before a turn

Trends rarely end with a bang. More often there is a moment of hesitation first: buyers and sellers stop pushing in the same direction, and the candles shrink. Candlestick "stars" capture that hesitation in one small candle.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Define a star and a doji star, and say what they tell you about the prevailing trend.
  • Identify a morning star and an evening star, and name the levels they create.
  • Explain the abandoned baby and how it differs from a plain doji star.
  • Judge when the strict rules can be relaxed (forex, indexes, intraday charts).
  • Plan an entry so the timing of the signal does not wreck your risk/reward.
Quick refresher The real body is the box between open and close. White (hollow) means the close was above the open; black (filled) means the close was below the open. The thin lines are the shadows (high and low).

What is a star?

A star is a candle with a small real body (white or black) that gaps away from the large real body just before it. The key test is about the bodies: they must not overlap. The star's shadow is allowed to dip into the prior candle's shadow range.

Why it matters

A long candle in the trend's direction shows one side firmly in control. The star shows that control has turned into a stalemate: either the dominant side is tiring or the other side is pushing back. Either way, the market is now more exposed to a pullback or reversal.

  • If the star's body is so small that open and close are about equal, it is a doji star, an even stronger warning.
  • A star in a downtrend is sometimes nicknamed a raindrop.
  • Stars are the middle ingredient in four reversal patterns: morning star, evening star, the doji stars, and the shooting star. This sample covers the first three.

The morning star (bottom reversal)

The name comes from the "morning star" that appears before sunrise: it hints that brighter (higher) prices may follow. It needs a prior downtrend and has three candles:

  1. Long black body. Sellers are clearly in charge.
  2. Small body that does not touch the first body. Sellers can no longer push prices down efficiently.
  3. White body that rises well into the first candle's black body. Buyers have taken over.

Quality checks

  • An ideal pattern shows a gap between candles 2 and 3 too, but its absence does not seem to weaken the signal in practice.
  • What matters most: candle 2 is a small, indecisive body (a spinning top) and candle 3 closes well into candle 1's body.
  • Volume helps: lighter volume on candle 1 and heavier volume on candle 3 suggests the old trend is fading while the new one gains force.
Candles can speak early A trendline or channel break is the classic Western signal that a downtrend has ended. A morning star can show up many sessions before that break, giving an earlier heads-up.

The evening star (top reversal)

The mirror image, named for the planet that shows up just before dark. It needs a prior uptrend:

  1. Long white body: buyers are in control.
  2. Star: the first hint of a top.
  3. Black body that closes sharply back into the first white body: confirmation that the trend has stalled.

Evening star vs. the Western island top

They look alike, but they are not identical tests. An island top needs the whole middle session, shadows included, to sit above the highs of the sessions on either side. An evening star only needs the middle real body to sit above the neighbouring bodies, so it can qualify when an island top would not.

What raises the odds (morning and evening stars)
  • No overlap among the three real bodies.
  • Candle 3 closes deep into candle 1's body.
  • Light volume on candle 1 and heavy volume on candle 3.
A bit of history The full Japanese names are "three-river" morning and evening stars. The label is traced to a famous campaign of the warlord Nobunaga Oda, who had to cross three rivers to take a rich rice-growing region. Hence the idea: once such a pattern forms, its zone is hard to cross, but a close beyond it signals that the attacker has won.

Doji stars and abandoned babies

When the middle candle is a doji (open and close nearly equal) instead of a small body, the patterns are called the morning doji star and evening doji star. The market has lost its directional bias entirely for that session, which makes the warning stronger.

The abandoned baby

If the doji's shadows do not touch the shadows of either neighbour (a true gap before and after it), the pattern is an abandoned baby: a top if it follows an uptrend, a bottom if it follows a downtrend. It is the candlestick version of a Western island top or bottom whose "island" is a doji, and it is very rare.

A near miss counts for something. If the middle candle is tiny enough to be read as a doji, the pattern can be treated as a close variation, even when the middle candle is also a hammer.

When the strict rules can bend

The "bodies must not touch" rule assumes a clear gap between one session's close and the next session's open. Some markets rarely give you that:

  • Foreign exchange: no official open or close.
  • Many indexes: the open is usually very close to the previous close.
  • Intraday charts: on a short timeframe (say 15 minutes) the new bar opens almost exactly where the last one closed.

In these cases you can accept a morning or evening star whose bodies touch end to end, and give more weight to context.

Context adds credibility In the intraday case, the pattern becomes more convincing if the last candle also pushes price back above a support level that had just been broken. A market that makes a new low and cannot hold it is often hinting at a reversal.

Trading stars: timing and risk

Stars are three-candle patterns, so you can only call them complete at the close of candle 3. By then price has often moved a long way from the extreme, which can make the trade unattractive: your stop sits far away, but your reward may be small.

A practical option

  1. Let the pattern complete and note its extreme (the support or resistance zone it created).
  2. Instead of chasing, wait for a pullback toward that zone. There is no guarantee one will come.
  3. Enter near the zone with your stop just beyond the pattern's extreme. A tighter stop improves the ratio.

Reversal patterns work best alongside other evidence: failed rallies at the evening star's high, a shrinking of real bodies near the turn, or a round-number level such as a "century" mark can all add confirmation.
