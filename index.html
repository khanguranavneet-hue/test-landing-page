Trading / 01.01 / Reversal patterns

Read the pause before the turn.

Trends rarely end with a bang. First comes hesitation: buyers and sellers stop pushing the same way, and the candles shrink. A star captures that hesitation in a single small candle.

Quick refresher The real body is the box between open and close. White (hollow) means the close was above the open. Black (filled) means the close was below the open. The thin lines are the shadows: the high and the low.

02Concept

What is a star?

A star is a candle with a small real body (white or black) that gaps away from the large real body just before it. The test is about the bodies: they must not overlap. The star's shadow may still dip into the prior candle's shadow range.

White (hollow): close above open Black (filled): close below open

Why it matters

A long candle in the trend's direction shows one side firmly in control. The star shows that control has turned into a stalemate: the dominant side is tiring or the other side is pushing back. Either way, the market is more exposed to a pullback or a reversal.

  • If the star's open and close are about equal, it is a doji star, an even stronger warning.
  • Stars are the middle ingredient of several reversal patterns. This lesson covers the morning star, the evening star and their doji versions.
03Pattern · bottom reversal

The morning star

Named for the star that shines before sunrise: a hint that brighter, higher prices may follow. It needs a prior downtrend and has three candles:

  1. Long black body. Sellers are clearly in charge.
  2. Small body that does not touch the first body. Sellers can no longer push prices down efficiently.
  3. White body that rises well into the first candle's black body. Buyers have taken over.

Quality checks

  • An ideal pattern also shows a gap between candles 2 and 3, but its absence does not seem to weaken the signal much in practice.
  • What matters most: candle 2 is a small, indecisive body, and candle 3 closes well into candle 1's body.
  • Volume helps: lighter on candle 1 and heavier on candle 3 suggests the old trend is fading while the new one gains force.
Candles can speak early A trendline or channel break is the classic Western sign that a downtrend has ended. A morning star can appear many sessions before that break, so it can give you an earlier heads-up.
04Pattern · top reversal

The evening star

The mirror image, named for the planet that shows up just before dark. It needs a prior uptrend:

  1. Long white body. Buyers are in control.
  2. Star. The first hint of a top.
  3. Black body that closes sharply back into the first white body. The trend has stalled.

Evening star vs. the Western island top

They look alike, but the tests differ. An island top needs the whole middle session, shadows included, to sit above the highs on either side. An evening star only needs the middle real body to sit above the neighbouring bodies, so it can qualify when an island top would not.

What raises the odds (morning and evening stars)
  • No overlap among the three real bodies.
  • Candle 3 closes deep into candle 1's body.
  • Light volume on candle 1 and heavy volume on candle 3.
A bit of history The full Japanese names are "three-river" morning and evening stars. The label is traced to a campaign of the warlord Nobunaga Oda, who had to cross three rivers to take a rich rice-growing region. Hence the idea: once such a pattern forms, its zone is hard to cross, but a close beyond it signals that the attacker has won.
05Variations

Doji stars and abandoned babies

When the middle candle is a doji (open and close nearly equal) instead of a small body, the patterns are the morning doji star and evening doji star. For that session the market has lost its directional bias entirely, which makes the warning stronger.

The abandoned baby

If the doji's shadows do not touch the shadows of either neighbour (a true gap before and after it), the pattern is an abandoned baby: a top after an uptrend, a bottom after a downtrend. It is the candlestick version of a Western island top or bottom whose "island" is a doji, and it is very rare.

A near miss still counts for something. If the middle candle is tiny enough to read as a doji, treat the pattern as a close variation, even when that candle is also a hammer.

06Practice

When the strict rules can bend

The "bodies must not touch" rule assumes a clear gap between one session's close and the next session's open. Some markets rarely give you that:

  • Foreign exchange: there is no official open or close.
  • Many indexes: the open is usually very close to the previous close.
  • Intraday charts: on a short timeframe (say 15 minutes) a new bar opens almost exactly where the last one closed.

In these cases you can accept a morning or evening star whose bodies touch end to end, and lean harder on context.

Context adds credibility In the intraday case, the pattern is more convincing if the last candle also pushes price back above a level that had just been broken. A market that makes a new low and cannot hold it is often hinting at a reversal.
07Risk

Timing and risk

Stars are three-candle patterns, so you can call one complete only at the close of candle 3. By then price has often moved a long way from the extreme. Your stop sits far away while your reward may be small. Know the risk before you chase the return.

A practical option

  1. Let the pattern complete and note its extreme: the support or resistance zone it created.
  2. Instead of chasing, wait for a pullback toward that zone. There is no guarantee one will come.
  3. Plan the entry near the zone with the stop just beyond the pattern's extreme. A tighter stop improves the ratio.

Reversal patterns work best alongside other evidence: failed rallies at an evening star's high, shrinking real bodies near the turn, or a round-number level such as a "century" mark.
